Every paper I've written sits on top of one unexamined assumption: that a vector of numbers can *mean* something, and that the geometric relationships between those vectors — distances, directions, neighborhoods — track the semantic relationships between the things they represent. That assumption is doing more work than it gets credit for. It's also not obviously true, and where it quietly fails is exactly where zero-shot methods break. This post is the geometric picture I actually hold in my head when I think about representation learning, and a walk through how CAST, IP-SAE, RevCD, and SEER-ZSL each respond to a different piece of it.

## What this covers

- Why an embedding is a geometric object, not just a lookup table of numbers
- The manifold hypothesis, and what it means for a representation to be "unfolded"
- Interpolation vs. extrapolation on a manifold, and how that's exactly what CAST's semantic extrapolation residual is measuring
- Hubness as a concentration-of-measure artifact of high-dimensional space, and how IP-SAE's shared latent space avoids it
- The linear representation hypothesis — concepts as directions — and why CAST's closed-form weight transfer is a direct bet on it
- The Platonic representation hypothesis, and how it reframes what RevCD and SEER-ZSL are actually doing

## Embeddings are points, representations are geometry

An embedding is a function $e: \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}^d$ that takes something non-numeric — an image, a word, a class label — and maps it to a point in a vector space. That much is just plumbing. The interesting claim, the one every downstream use of an embedding depends on, is that the *geometry* of $\mathbb{R}^d$ under some notion of distance or direction reflects the *semantics* of $\mathcal{X}$: that similar things land near each other, that meaningful categories carve out coherent regions, and that the structure of the space can be manipulated (averaged, projected, transformed) and have the results still mean something.

None of that is guaranteed by the architecture. It's an empirical regularity that representation learning tries to induce, and the degree to which it holds — and how it fails — is best understood geometrically. Three ideas do most of the work: the space these embeddings actually occupy (the manifold hypothesis), the internal structure of that space (the linear representation hypothesis), and whether independently-learned spaces end up describing the *same* underlying geometry (the Platonic representation hypothesis).

## The manifold hypothesis

Real data doesn't fill its ambient space. A $224 \times 224$ RGB image lives in a $\mathbb{R}^{150{,}528}$-dimensional pixel space, but the set of images that look like *anything* — let alone a cat, or a class of birds — is an infinitesimal sliver of that space. The **manifold hypothesis** (Bengio, Courville & Vincent, 2013; formalized further by [Fefferman, Mitter & Narayanan, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1310.0425)) says that naturally occurring high-dimensional data concentrates near a much lower-dimensional manifold $\mathcal{M} \subset \mathbb{R}^D$, $\dim(\mathcal{M}) = k \ll D$, embedded curvily inside the ambient space. There's a smooth generative map $g: \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}^D$ from a small set of true underlying factors $z$ to the observed high-dimensional $x \approx g(z)$.

Under this view, representation learning is the act of finding a *chart* for that manifold — a map back down to something close to the intrinsic coordinates $z$, ideally one where the coordinate axes correspond to something interpretable. A good encoder doesn't just compress; it *unfolds* the curved manifold into something flatter and more linearly organized than the raw pixel space ever was.

<img src="/diagrams/manifold-unfolding.svg" alt="A curved data manifold sitting in a mostly-empty high-dimensional ambient space on the left, mapped by an encoder into a flatter, linearly organized representation on the right where the same points are spread along coordinates aligned with the underlying generative factors" />

This is precisely the mechanism [IP-SAE](/projects/ip-sae/) leans on: rather than trusting that the visual feature space and the semantic attribute space already agree on a geometry, it learns an encoder that projects the concatenation of both onto one shared latent manifold, with a decoder that reconstructs from it. The claim embedded in that architecture is a manifold-hypothesis claim — that visual and semantic descriptions of the same class are two noisy renderings of one shared low-dimensional structure, and that structure can be recovered by projection.

## Interpolation, extrapolation, and CAST's residual

Once you accept there's a manifold, the sharpest question you can ask about any point on it is: *how far is it from the data you've actually seen, measured along the manifold rather than through the ambient space?* This is the geodesic distance $d_\mathcal{M}(x, y)$, and it's a fundamentally different quantity from the Euclidean distance $\|x - y\|_2$ — two points can be Euclidean-close while requiring a long walk along the manifold to get from one to the other, if the manifold curves between them.

This distinction is the entire ballgame for zero-shot learning. A new class whose semantic embedding sits *inside* the convex region spanned by seen classes' embeddings is an interpolation problem — the model is being asked to produce something structurally similar to combinations of things it already understands. A new class whose embedding sits *outside* that region is extrapolation — genuinely novel structure, with no nearby seen examples to triangulate from.

<img src="/diagrams/manifold-geometry.svg" alt="A curved manifold in a high-dimensional embedding space with seen-class points along it, an unseen class close to the manifold representing interpolation, a second unseen class far from it representing extrapolation with a semantic extrapolation residual, and an off-manifold hub point that many nearest-neighbor queries incorrectly snap to" />

[CAST](/projects/cast/) makes this distinction explicit rather than implicit. It extends a frozen classifier to a new class by computing new weights directly from a semantic embedding, with no training and no images of the new class at all — which only works if there's a computable relationship between semantic geometry and classifier-weight geometry robust enough to trust at a brand-new point. CAST's theoretical contribution is an error decomposition that isolates exactly the term this section is about: the **semantic extrapolation residual**, the part of the extension error attributable to how far the new class's semantic embedding sits from the classes the model already knows. That's a geodesic-distance argument dressed up as an error bound — extension quality degrades precisely as you leave the interpolation regime and start extrapolating off the region the model has actually mapped.

## Hubness: a curse-of-dimensionality artifact

Not every geometric failure in embedding space is about *where* a point sits relative to the manifold. Some are about the ambient space itself misbehaving. In high dimensions, pairwise distances concentrate — as $D$ grows, the ratio between the nearest and farthest neighbor of a typical point tends toward 1, and ordinary Euclidean or cosine distance stops discriminating well between "similar" and "dissimilar." A specific, well-documented symptom of this is **hubness** ([Radovanović, Nanopoulos & Ivanović, 2010](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v11/radovanovic10a.html)): a small number of points become the nearest neighbor for a disproportionate share of queries, regardless of whether they're semantically related to those queries at all. It's a statistical artifact of high-dimensional geometry, not a semantic failure — but for anything built on nearest-neighbor retrieval, it's indistinguishable from one.

Zero-shot classification is exactly that kind of nearest-neighbor system: an unseen image gets matched to whichever class embedding it lands closest to. If a handful of semantic embeddings are geometric hubs, they absorb classifications that have nothing to do with them, independent of how good the underlying features are. This is the second problem [IP-SAE](/projects/ip-sae/) targets directly — its regularization on the projection discourages the encoder from collapsing many classes toward a small number of hub points, on top of the domain-invariance argument above. Two different diagnoses, one shared fix: constrain the learned manifold's geometry rather than trusting whatever shape falls out of concatenating two feature spaces.

## The linear representation hypothesis

Manifold geometry describes the *shape* of the space representations live in. The **linear representation hypothesis** is a much sharper claim about its *internal structure*: that individual concepts correspond to (roughly) linear directions in that space, and that combining concepts corresponds to vector addition. The canonical demonstration is decades old — $e(\text{king}) - e(\text{man}) + e(\text{woman}) \approx e(\text{queen})$ in word2vec — but it's been formalized considerably since, most directly in [Park, Choe & Veitch (2023)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658), who define what it means for a concept to be linearly represented in terms of a causal inner product on embedding space, and it's the operating assumption behind interpretability work like Anthropic's [Toy Models of Superposition](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2022/toy_model/index.html), which studies how far more concepts than dimensions can still be linearly encoded by exploiting sparsity.

<img src="/diagrams/linear-representation.svg" alt="A parallelogram in embedding space with man, woman, king, and queen at the four corners, showing that the offset from man to king is parallel and equal in length to the offset from woman to queen, illustrating the vector arithmetic e(king) minus e(man) plus e(woman) approximately equals e(queen)" />

This hypothesis is what makes CAST's method possible at all, not just its error bound. Injecting new classifier weights directly from a semantic embedding, in closed form, with no gradient steps, requires that there be a *computable* — and in CAST's case, analytically closed-form — transfer from semantic-embedding geometry to classifier-weight geometry. That's only a sane thing to attempt if the map between those two spaces is well-behaved enough to be approximated linearly (or affinely) over local regions: a direction in semantic space needs to correspond to a consistent, composable direction in weight space. Where that local linearity holds, the closed-form transfer works cheaply and well. Where it doesn't — far enough into extrapolation — is exactly where the semantic extrapolation residual from the previous section starts to dominate. The linear representation hypothesis and CAST's error decomposition are two views of the same boundary.

## The Platonic representation hypothesis

The manifold and linear hypotheses are both claims about a *single* representation space. The **Platonic representation hypothesis** ([Huh, Cheung, Wang & Isola, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07987)) is a claim about many of them at once: that representations learned by different models, trained on different data, even in different modalities, are converging toward a shared statistical model of reality — an idealized representation that each model's embedding is an imperfect, differently-rotated projection of, in the spirit of Plato's forms. The evidence is empirical: representational alignment between independently trained vision and language models increases with scale and data coverage, as if every sufficiently capable model is grasping toward the same underlying object from a different angle.

<img src="/diagrams/platonic-representation.svg" alt="Three separate embedding spaces — visual embeddings, semantic attribute embeddings, and noisy modality-specific descriptions — each connected by an alignment transform to a shared central representation Z, illustrating the Platonic representation hypothesis that independently trained models converge on one underlying statistical model of reality" />

This reframes what [RevCD](/projects/revcd/) and [SEER-ZSL](/projects/seer-zsl/) are doing. The standard generative zero-shot setup assumes a *fixed, one-directional* mapping from semantic description to visual appearance — semantics as the cause, visuals as the rendering. RevCD inverts the conditioning and generates the semantic embedding *from* visual attention instead. That inversion only makes sense under something like the Platonic view: if semantic and visual embeddings were genuinely two independent, unrelated objects, there'd be no reason inferring one from the other should work better than assuming a fixed correspondence. But if both are noisy, modality-specific projections of one shared underlying concept — what the class actually *is*, independent of whether you're looking at it or reading about it — then treating the semantic embedding as something to be *inferred* from visual evidence, rather than fixed in advance, is the more faithful model. That's the rigidity RevCD is built to relax.

SEER-ZSL sits on the same idea from the other side. Real-world semantic descriptions are noisy, inconsistent, and often automatically generated — a degraded rendering of some more coherent underlying structure. SEER-ZSL's probabilistic semantic encoder is explicitly trying to distill that structure back out before using it to bridge visual and semantic space. In geometric terms: if there's a shared Platonic representation underneath both modalities, then the noisy semantic embedding you're handed is a corrupted observation of a point near it, and a probabilistic encoder that denoises toward more consistent structure is a step toward recovering something closer to that shared object — rather than just fitting whatever idiosyncratic space the raw semantic features happen to define.

## One geometric problem, four different fixes

Read this way, all four papers are answers to the same underlying question — *the seen-class manifold you have access to during training isn't the same object as the true concept manifold you're trying to generalize over, so what do you do about the gap?* — with each one attacking a different facet of that gap:

- **IP-SAE** reshapes the ambient geometry so visual and semantic features land on one shared, domain-invariant manifold in the first place, and regularizes it against hub collapse.
- **RevCD** stops assuming a fixed, rigid correspondence between two projections of that manifold, and infers one from the other instead — a Platonic move.
- **SEER-ZSL** tries to recover a cleaner version of the shared structure from noisy, modality-specific descriptions of it — the same Platonic move, applied before the semantic embedding is even used.
- **CAST** measures and exploits the *local linear structure* of that manifold directly, extending a classifier with no training at all where the linear representation hypothesis holds, and characterizing exactly how error grows as you leave the region where it does.

## Key takeaway

None of "manifold," "linear representation," or "Platonic representation" is a settled fact about how neural networks work — they're hypotheses, useful mostly because they make sharp, falsifiable predictions about geometry: that data concentrates on a low-dimensional surface, that concepts are directions rather than arbitrary regions, and that independently trained models converge on a shared underlying structure rather than inventing unrelated ones. But as working hypotheses, they're unreasonably good at explaining *why* a given representation-learning method should work before you've run a single experiment — and, just as usefully, at predicting exactly where and how it will fail. Zero-shot learning is a particularly clean testbed for this because it removes the safety net: there's no training data to paper over a bad geometric assumption at the class you actually care about. Every method in this space is, whether it says so or not, a bet on some piece of this geometric picture.
